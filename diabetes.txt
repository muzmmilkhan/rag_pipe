DIABETES: TYPES, TESTING, AND EVERYDAY MANAGEMENT

What is diabetes?

Diabetes mellitus is a group of conditions that cause blood glucose (blood sugar) to remain higher than normal. Glucose is an important source of energy. Insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas, helps glucose move from the bloodstream into cells. Diabetes develops when the body does not make enough insulin, cannot use insulin effectively, or both.

The main types

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas. The body then makes little or no insulin. People with type 1 diabetes need insulin every day. It can begin at any age and is not caused by eating too much sugar.

Type 2 diabetes occurs when the body becomes less responsive to insulin and, over time, may not make enough to keep blood glucose in a healthy range. It is influenced by a combination of genetic, biological, and environmental factors. It is the most common type. Treatment may include changes to daily habits, medicines, and sometimes insulin.

Gestational diabetes is diabetes first recognized during pregnancy. Pregnancy-related changes can make it harder for the body to use insulin. It often improves after birth, but follow-up testing is important because gestational diabetes increases the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later.

Other, less common forms exist, including diabetes caused by certain diseases, medicines, or inherited conditions. A healthcare professional can help identify the type because treatment depends on the cause.

Symptoms and risk

Possible symptoms include increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurry vision, unexplained weight loss, and slow-healing cuts or infections. Type 2 diabetes can develop gradually and may cause few noticeable symptoms. Some people discover it through routine blood tests.

Risk factors differ by diabetes type. Family history, age, prior gestational diabetes, and some health conditions can increase the likelihood of type 2 diabetes. Risk factors do not determine with certainty whether someone will develop it, and people without obvious risk factors can also be affected.

How diabetes is diagnosed

Clinicians use blood tests to diagnose diabetes. Common tests include A1C, which estimates average blood glucose over roughly the previous two to three months; fasting plasma glucose; and an oral glucose tolerance test. A random blood glucose test may also be used when symptoms are present.

Diagnostic cutoffs depend on the test and applicable clinical guidelines. Unless blood glucose is clearly very high with typical symptoms, an abnormal result is generally confirmed with a repeat test or another diagnostic test. A clinician interprets results in context; a home glucose meter alone does not establish a diagnosis.

Managing diabetes

Diabetes management is individualized. It may include monitoring blood glucose, choosing a sustainable eating pattern, regular physical activity, adequate sleep, and prescribed medicines. Type 1 diabetes requires insulin. Type 2 diabetes treatment can include several kinds of medicines, and some people also use insulin.

There is no single meal plan that is right for everyone. Many people benefit from regular meals that include vegetables, high-fiber foods, and suitable sources of protein, while limiting sugary drinks. Personal preferences, culture, other health conditions, medicines, and access to food all matter. A registered dietitian or diabetes care team can help tailor a plan.

Physical activity can help the body use insulin and support overall health. The right activity and safety precautions depend on a person's health, medicines, and glucose patterns. People who use insulin or medicines that can cause low blood glucose may need a plan for checking and treating low levels during or after exercise.

Monitoring may involve a blood glucose meter or a continuous glucose monitor. A1C testing can help assess longer-term glucose management. How often to monitor and what targets to use should be decided with a healthcare professional.

Possible complications

Over time, persistently high blood glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves. Diabetes can increase the risk of heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, eye disease, nerve problems, and foot problems. Regular checkups, glucose management, and attention to blood pressure and cholesterol can help reduce risks. The right screening schedule depends on the person and their type of diabetes.

Low blood glucose

Low blood glucose, also called hypoglycemia, can occur with insulin and some other diabetes medicines. Symptoms may include shakiness, sweating, hunger, dizziness, irritability, or confusion. Severe hypoglycemia can cause seizures or loss of consciousness.

People at risk should ask their care team for a personal plan for recognizing and treating low blood glucose. If someone is unconscious or unable to swallow safely, do not give food or drink by mouth. Use prescribed glucagon if available and seek emergency medical help.

When to seek urgent help

Very high or very low blood glucose can become dangerous. Seek urgent medical care for severe confusion, fainting, seizures, difficulty breathing, or repeated vomiting, especially in someone with diabetes. Follow the person's emergency care plan and contact local emergency services when symptoms are severe.

Prevention and common misconceptions

Type 1 diabetes cannot currently be prevented through diet or exercise. For some people at increased risk of type 2 diabetes, lifestyle support and, in selected cases, medication can delay or prevent its onset. Prevention strategies are not a guarantee and should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

Diabetes is not simply caused by eating sugar. Sugary foods and drinks can affect blood glucose and overall health, but diabetes has multiple causes. People with diabetes can often include a range of foods in a suitable eating plan; individual guidance is more useful than blanket food bans.

Quick facts

- Diabetes is a group of conditions involving problems with insulin and blood glucose.
- Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune and requires insulin treatment.
- Type 2 diabetes involves insulin resistance and can have several contributing factors.
- Gestational diabetes is first recognized during pregnancy and requires follow-up after birth.
- Blood tests, interpreted by a healthcare professional, are used for diagnosis.
- Diabetes care is individualized; there is no one-size-fits-all meal or treatment plan.
- This document provides general education and is not a substitute for personal medical advice.
